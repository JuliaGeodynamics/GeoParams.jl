Magma suspensions ​

Suspension wraps the creep law of a melt and multiplies its viscosity by the relative viscosities ηr=η/ηmelt of suspended crystals and bubbles. The crystal and gas volume fractions of the whole suspension are passed at runtime as ϕ_x and ϕ_gas:

julia
using GeoParams, CairoMakie
law = Suspension(LinearViscous(η = 1.0e3Pa * s); crystals = Roscoe1952_Crystals(), bubbles = IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles())
compute_relative_viscosity(law; ϕ_x = 0.3, ϕ_gas = 0.1)
4.272227540300969

Rigid crystals and undeformable bubbles ​

Roscoe1952_Crystals describes rigid particles that lock up at the maximum packing fraction ϕmax, and IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles describes bubbles that stay spherical. The figure compares Roscoe's (1952) parameters for equal spheres, the Krieger–Dougherty form fitted to crystal-bearing basalt by Keller & Suckale (2019), and Ishii–Zuber bubbles as a function of their volume fraction.

julia
ϕ = range(0, 0.74, 300)
fig = Figure(size = (800, 500))
ax = Axis(fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "crystal or gas volume fraction ϕ (–)", ylabel = "relative viscosity ηᵣ (–)", yscale = log10)
lines!(ax, ϕ, compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(Roscoe1952_Crystals()), ϕ); label = "Roscoe1952_Crystals(), Roscoe (1952)")
lines!(ax, ϕ, compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(Roscoe1952_Crystals(ϕ_max = 0.58, n = 2.32)), ϕ);
    label = "Roscoe1952_Crystals(ϕ_max = 0.58, n = 2.32), Keller & Suckale (2019)")
lines!(ax, ϕ, compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles()), ϕ); label = "IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles() (bubbles)")
ylims!(ax, 1, 1.0e4)
axislegend(ax; position = :lt)
fig

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.Suspension Type
julia
Suspension(law; crystals = nothing, bubbles = nothing)

Creep law law of the melt, stiffened by suspended crystals and bubbles. As in the three-phase model of Truby et al. (2015, eq. 3.2), the crystals are suspended in the bubbly melt:

ε˙II(τII)=ε˙IIlaw(τII)ηr,x(ϕx)ηr,g(ϕb),ϕb=ϕgas1−ϕx,

where ϕb is the gas fraction of the bubbly melt (eqs 3.4, 3.5 and 3.7), so a Newtonian melt viscosity ηmelt becomes η=ηmeltηr,xηr,g. A melt law with stress exponent n is evaluated at ηr,xηr,gε˙II, so its viscosity grows by (ηr,xηr,g)1/n. Truby et al. validate this for spherical particles in the Maron–Pierce law and undeformed bubbles (IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles) up to ϕx≈0.5 and ϕb≈0.3. crystals and bubbles are relative viscosities such as Roscoe1952_Crystals, Mueller2010_Crystals and IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles; nothing means no effect.

The relative viscosities may also depend on the strain rate ε˙II of the suspension. Crystal laws such as Vona2011_Crystals depend on its shear rate 2ε˙II. Bubble laws such as Mader2013_Bubbles depend on the capillary number, which the stress of the melt sets at the strain rate ηr,x/(1−ϕx)ε˙II of the bubbly melt, the average melt strain rate that dissipates the same energy as the crystal suspension (Chateau et al. 2008). Then compute_τII stays explicit, and compute_εII solves for ε˙II by Newton iteration and returns NaN if it does not converge. At high gas fractions the stress of capillary bubble laws can decrease with strain rate, so the strain rate at a given stress need not be unique.

The crystal and gas volume fractions of the whole suspension are passed at runtime as ϕ_x and ϕ_gas, the same keywords that ThreePhase_Density uses, e.g. compute_εII(law, τII; T, ϕ_x = 0.3, ϕ_gas = 0.1). Both are required. All keyword arguments are also passed on to law.

Example

julia
julia> law = Suspension(LinearViscous(η = 1e5Pa*s); crystals = Roscoe1952_Crystals(ϕ_max = 1), bubbles = IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles());

julia> compute_τII(law, 1.0; ϕ_x = 0.2, ϕ_gas = 0.5) / compute_τII(law.law, 1.0)
4.658474953124561

References

  • Chateau, X., Ovarlez, G., & Trung, K. L. (2008). Homogenization approach to the behavior of suspensions of noncolloidal particles in yield stress fluids. Journal of Rheology, 52(2), 489–506. https://doi.org/10.1122/1.2838254

  • Truby, J. M., Mueller, S. P., Llewellin, E. W., & Mader, H. M. (2015). The rheology of three-phase suspensions at low bubble capillary number. Proceedings of the Royal Society A, 471(2173), 20140557. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.2014.0557

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.Roscoe1952_Crystals Type
julia
Roscoe1952_Crystals(; ϕ_max = 1/1.35, n = 2.5)

Einstein–Roscoe relative viscosity of a suspension of rigid spheres,

ηr=(1−ϕxϕmax)−n,

with ϕx the crystal volume fraction. Roscoe (1952) uses n=5/2 and gives ϕmax=1/1.35 for equal spheres at high concentration and ϕmax=1 for spheres of very different sizes. The Krieger & Dougherty (1959) form has n=Bϕmax with Einstein coefficient B; Keller & Suckale (2019) fit ϕmax=0.58 and B=4 (n=2.32) to crystal-bearing basalt. ηr diverges at ϕx=ϕmax and is NaN outside 0≤ϕx≤ϕmax.

References

  • Roscoe, R. (1952). The viscosity of suspensions of rigid spheres. British Journal of Applied Physics, 3(8), 267–269. https://doi.org/10.1088/0508-3443/3/8/306

  • Krieger, I. M., & Dougherty, T. J. (1959). A mechanism for non-Newtonian flow in suspensions of rigid spheres. Transactions of the Society of Rheology, 3, 137–152. https://doi.org/10.1122/1.548848

  • Keller, T., & Suckale, J. (2019). A continuum model of multi-phase reactive transport in igneous systems. Geophysical Journal International, 219(1), 185–222. https://doi.org/10.1093/gji/ggz287

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles Type
julia
IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles()

Ishii–Zuber relative viscosity of a suspension of undeformable spherical bubbles (capillary number Ca≪1),

ηr=11−ϕgas,

with ϕgas the gas volume fraction. This is the Ishii & Zuber (1979) mixture viscosity for an inviscid dispersed phase with maximum packing 1. ηr is NaN outside 0≤ϕgas≤1.

References

  • Ishii, M., & Zuber, N. (1979). Drag coefficient and relative velocity in bubbly, droplet or particulate flows. AIChE Journal, 25(5), 843–855. https://doi.org/10.1002/aic.690250513

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.compute_relative_viscosity Function
julia
compute_relative_viscosity(f::AbstractRateDependentViscosity, ϕ, x)

Relative viscosity of the law f at volume fraction ϕ and rate x: the capillary number for bubble laws such as Mader2013_Bubbles, and the shear rate γ˙=2ε˙II for Vona2011_Crystals.

Example

julia
julia> compute_relative_viscosity(Mader2013_Bubbles(), 0.5, 0.0)
2.0
source
julia
compute_relative_viscosity(f::AbstractRelativeViscosity, ϕ)
compute_relative_viscosity(a::Suspension; ϕ_x, ϕ_gas)
compute_relative_viscosity(a::Suspension, args)

Relative viscosity ηr=η/ηmelt of the law f at volume fraction ϕ, or of the suspension a, which is ηr,x(ϕx)ηr,g(ϕgas/(1−ϕx)). args is a NamedTuple that must contain ϕ_x and ϕ_gas; its other entries are ignored.

Example

julia
julia> compute_relative_viscosity(IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles(), 0.5)
2.0

Particle shape, shear rate and bubble deformation ​

The laws of this section account for the shape of crystals and for the dependence of ηr on the strain rate of the suspension.

Elongated crystals ​

Mueller2010_Crystals lowers the maximum packing fraction with the particle aspect ratio rp, so elongated crystals stiffen the melt at much lower crystal fractions than spheres. The curve for rp=10 extrapolates slightly beyond the fitted range 1≤rp≤9.17.

julia
using GeoParams, CairoMakie
ϕ_x = range(0, 0.74, 300)
fig = Figure(size = (800, 500))
ax = Axis(fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "crystal volume fraction ϕₓ (–)", ylabel = "relative viscosity ηᵣ (–)", yscale = log10)
for r_p in (1, 5, 10)
    lines!(ax, ϕ_x, compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(Mueller2010_Crystals(aspect_ratio = r_p)), ϕ_x); label = "Mueller2010_Crystals, rₚ = $r_p")
end
lines!(ax, ϕ_x, compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(Roscoe1952_Crystals()), ϕ_x); color = :black, linestyle = :dash,
    label = "Roscoe1952_Crystals(), Roscoe (1952)")
ylims!(ax, 1, 1.0e4)
axislegend(ax; position = :lt)
fig

Shear-thinning crystal suspensions ​

Vona2011_Crystals makes the Maron–Pierce law of Mueller2010_Crystals shear thinning: ηr decreases with the shear rate γ˙=2ε˙II and equals the Mueller et al. (2010) value at γ˙=1 s⁻¹. The figure shows ηr relative to that value. The law was calibrated for 0.07≤ϕx≤0.27 (shaded) and shear rates of 0.02–2.2 s⁻¹; elsewhere it is an extrapolation.

julia
ϕ_x = range(0, 0.35, 300)
f = Vona2011_Crystals()
η_r(ϕ, γ̇) = compute_relative_viscosity(f, ϕ, γ̇)
fig = Figure(size = (800, 500))
ax = Axis(fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "crystal volume fraction ϕₓ (–)", ylabel = "ηᵣ(γ̇) / ηᵣ(1 s⁻¹) (–)")
vspan!(ax, 0.07, 0.27; color = (:gray, 0.2), label = "calibrated range of ϕₓ")
hlines!(ax, 1; color = :black, linestyle = :dash, label = "γ̇ = 1 s⁻¹ (Mueller2010_Crystals, rₚ = 6.5)")
for γ̇ in (0.02, 0.2, 2.0)
    lines!(ax, ϕ_x, η_r.(ϕ_x, γ̇) ./ η_r.(ϕ_x, 1.0); label = "Vona2011_Crystals(), γ̇ = $γ̇ s⁻¹")
end
xlims!(ax, 0, 0.35)
axislegend(ax; position = :lt)
fig

Deformable bubbles ​

Bubbles stiffen the melt while surface tension keeps them spherical and weaken it once the flow stretches them. Mader2013_Bubbles interpolates between the two limits as a function of the capillary number Ca (see AbstractCapillaryViscosity): ηr0=1/(1−ϕ), which equals IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles, for Ca→0 (dashed), and ηr∞=(1−ϕ)5/3 for Ca→∞ (dotted).

julia
f = Mader2013_Bubbles()
Ca = 10 .^ range(-2, 2, 300)
fig = Figure(size = (800, 500))
ax = Axis(fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "capillary number Ca (–)", ylabel = "relative viscosity ηᵣ (–)", xscale = log10)
ϕ_gas = (0.1, 0.3, 0.5)
colors = Makie.wong_colors()[1:3]
for (ϕ, color) in zip(ϕ_gas, colors)
    lines!(ax, Ca, compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(f), ϕ, Ca); color)
    hlines!(ax, compute_relative_viscosity(IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles(), ϕ); color, linestyle = :dash)
    hlines!(ax, (1 - ϕ)^(5 / 3); color, linestyle = :dot)
end
elements = [[LineElement(; color) for color in colors]; LineElement(color = :black, linestyle = :dash);
    LineElement(color = :black, linestyle = :dot)]
labels = [["Mader2013_Bubbles(), ϕ_gas = $ϕ" for ϕ in ϕ_gas]; "Ca → 0: (1 − ϕ)⁻¹ (IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles)"; "Ca → ∞: (1 − ϕ)^(5/3)"]
axislegend(ax, elements, labels; position = :rt)
fig

Stress decreasing with strain rate

For a Newtonian melt, the capillary number is proportional to the strain rate, so the stress of the suspension is proportional to ηr(Ca)Ca. With y=(KCa)2 its slope, ηr∞+(ηr0−ηr∞)(1−y)/(1+y)2, is smallest at y=3, where it equals ηr∞−(ηr0−ηr∞)/8. It is negative if 9ηr∞<ηr0, i.e. (1−ϕ)8/3<1/9 or ϕ>1−9−3/8≈0.561, where ϕ is the gas fraction ϕgas/(1−ϕx) of the bubbly melt. The stress then decreases with strain rate over a range of strain rates, which makes the momentum equation ill-posed and the strain rate at a given stress non-unique.

The threshold can be checked numerically:

julia
stress_decreases(ϕ_gas) = any(diff(compute_relative_viscosity.(Ref(Mader2013_Bubbles()), ϕ_gas, Ca) .* Ca) .< 0)
stress_decreases(0.55), stress_decreases(0.57)
(false, true)

Three-phase suspensions ​

Following Truby et al. (2015), Suspension suspends the crystals in the bubbly melt, whose gas fraction is ϕb=ϕgas/(1−ϕx). At constant ϕgas the bubbles therefore stiffen the magma more as the crystal fraction grows. Truby et al. validated this combination for spheres (rp=1) and undeformed bubbles up to ϕx≈0.5 and ϕb≈0.3, the range plotted here.

julia
law = Suspension(LinearViscous(); crystals = Mueller2010_Crystals(aspect_ratio = 1), bubbles = IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles())
ϕ_x = range(0, 0.5, 300)
fig = Figure(size = (800, 500))
ax = Axis(fig[1, 1]; xlabel = "crystal volume fraction ϕₓ (–)", ylabel = "relative viscosity ηᵣ (–)", yscale = log10)
for ϕ_gas in (0.0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15)
    lines!(ax, ϕ_x, [compute_relative_viscosity(law; ϕ_x = x, ϕ_gas) for x in ϕ_x]; label = "ϕ_gas = $ϕ_gas")
end
axislegend(ax; position = :lt)
fig

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.Mueller2010_Crystals Function
julia
Mueller2010_Crystals(; aspect_ratio)

Maron–Pierce relative viscosity of a suspension of particles with aspect ratio rp = aspect_ratio, after Mueller et al. (2010, eqs 2.4 and 5.1),

ηr=(1−ϕxϕm)−2,ϕm=20.321rp+3.02,

returned as the equivalent Roscoe1952_Crystals law with ϕmax=ϕm and n=2. The fit of ϕm covers spheres and prolate particles, 1≤rp≤9.17. Mueller et al. (2010) describe their suspensions as Herschel–Bulkley fluids; this law is their consistency, which equals ηr at a shear rate of 1 s⁻¹ only for suspensions without yield stress, and omits their shear thinning (eq. 5.2) and yield stress (eq. 4.1).

References

  • Mueller, S., Llewellin, E. W., & Mader, H. M. (2010). The rheology of suspensions of solid particles. Proceedings of the Royal Society A, 466(2116), 1201–1228. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.2009.0445

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.Vona2011_Crystals Type
julia
Vona2011_Crystals(; aspect_ratio = 6.5, a = 0.06)

Shear-thinning relative viscosity of crystal-bearing basalt after Vona et al. (2011, eq. 23),

ηr=(1−ϕxϕm)−2[1−alog10⁡(γ˙/γ˙0)],γ˙0=1s−1,

with crystal volume fraction ϕx, shear rate γ˙ and the maximum packing fraction ϕm of Mueller2010_Crystals for crystals of mean aspect ratio aspect_ratio, as Vona et al. (2011, eq. 6) compute it. Equivalently, the suspension is a power-law fluid, ηr=(1−ϕx/ϕm)−2(γ˙/γ˙0)n−1 with flow index n=1+2alog10⁡(1−ϕx/ϕm) (eq. 20), whose consistency is the Maron–Pierce law. Vona et al. fit a=0.06 to Stromboli and Etna basalts with 0.07≤ϕx≤0.27, mean aspect ratios 5.6–7.3 and shear rates 0.02–2.2 s⁻¹ in a concentric-cylinder viscometer. That shear rate of simple shear is γ˙=2ε˙II; in Suspension it is the shear rate of the whole suspension. The default aspect ratio is the representative value of their Fig. 10b. ηr depends on |γ˙| and is held at its value at |γ˙|=10−30γ˙0 below that rate, so it is finite at γ˙=0. It is NaN outside 0≤ϕx≤ϕm.

Example

julia
julia> compute_relative_viscosity(Vona2011_Crystals(aspect_ratio = 7.25), 0.272, 0.02)
17.515418342481468

References

  • Vona, A., Romano, C., Dingwell, D. B., & Giordano, D. (2011). The rheology of crystal-bearing basaltic magmas from Stromboli and Etna. Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta, 75(11), 3214–3236. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2011.03.031

GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.AbstractCapillaryViscosity Type
julia
AbstractCapillaryViscosity

Relative viscosity ηr(ϕ,Ca) of a suspension of deformable bubbles, which also depends on the capillary number

Ca=ηγ˙rΓ=rτIIΓ,

with bubble radius r, surface tension Γ and the viscosity η of the liquid around the bubbles. The shear rate γ˙=2ε˙II equals 2ε˙ijε˙ij and is the engineering shear rate in simple shear, so ηγ˙ is the second stress invariant τII of that liquid.

source
GeoParams.MaterialParameters.ConstitutiveRelationships.Mader2013_Bubbles Type
julia
Mader2013_Bubbles(; r = 1e-4m, Γ = 0.3J/m^2)

Relative viscosity of a suspension of deformable bubbles in steady flow, the Cross model of Mader et al. (2013, eqs 27–29),

ηr=ηr∞+ηr0−ηr∞1+(KCa)2,ηr0=11−ϕgas,ηr∞=(1−ϕgas)5/3,K=65,

with gas volume fraction ϕgas, bubble radius r and surface tension Γ. K and the exponent 2 are from Llewellin et al. (2002); the limits ηr0 and ηr∞ are those of Llewellin & Manga (2005, eqs 7–8), who switch between them at Ca=1. The capillary number Ca is defined in AbstractCapillaryViscosity. Undeformed bubbles (Ca→0) stiffen the melt as in IshiiZuber1979_Bubbles; fully deformed bubbles (Ca→∞) weaken it. For dilute suspensions the law reduces to ηr=1+ϕgas(1−125Ca2)/(1+3625Ca2), eq. 11 of Llewellin et al. (2002) with the theoretical (Taylor) dilute value b=1; Llewellin et al. (2002) fit b=9 to their data. ηr is NaN outside 0≤ϕgas≤1.

The default Γ = 0.3 N/m is the value of the worked example of Mader et al. (2013, §7.3.1, p. 155), the upper end of the range 0.05–0.3 N/m they give for hydrous to anhydrous silicate melts (§3.1, p. 137). The default bubble radius r = 0.1 mm is a placeholder; set it for each application.

References

  • Mader, H. M., Llewellin, E. W., & Mueller, S. P. (2013). The rheology of two-phase magmas: A review and analysis. Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research, 257, 135–158. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvolgeores.2013.02.014

  • Llewellin, E. W., Mader, H. M., & Wilson, S. D. R. (2002). The constitutive equation and flow dynamics of bubbly magmas. Geophysical Research Letters, 29(24), 2170. https://doi.org/10.1029/2002GL015697

  • Llewellin, E. W., & Manga, M. (2005). Bubble suspension rheology and implications for conduit flow. Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research, 143(1–3), 205–217. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvolgeores.2004.09.018
